Correlations — Find out more

This page describes how correlations between input parameters affect Monte Carlo simulation results in the tool, what the two correlation cards on the Simulate tab control, and how to interpret each option using two worked examples.

The principle: when two input parameters tend to move together in nature, the Monte Carlo sampler should reproduce that joint behaviour. Sampling them independently when they are correlated produces parameter combinations that do not occur in real systems, which biases the spread (though not the central estimate) of the simulated emissions distribution.

Visualising correlation

Consider two parameters, with 1000 Monte Carlo samples of each, plotted as a scatter cloud — one point per iteration, the first parameter on the x-axis and the second on the y-axis. The three panels below show the same two marginal distributions under different correlation strengths:

In the centre panel, when parameter 1 takes a high value, parameter 2 tends to be high as well. The cloud tilts upward. In the right-hand panel the relationship is reversed: high values of parameter 1 occur with low values of parameter 2, and the cloud tilts downward.

The marginal distribution of each parameter is identical across all three panels — the same range on each axis. Correlation does not change the distribution of either parameter on its own; it changes only which combinations of values occur together.

Preserving marginal distributions (Iman–Conover method)

Real inventory parameters rarely follow symmetric normal distributions. Body weight is approximately symmetric, milk yield has a long right tail, and the methane conversion factor Ym is bounded between approximately 5% and 8%. A bivariate-normal sampler with a target correlation cannot reproduce these shapes.

The tool uses the Iman–Conover procedure (Iman & Conover 1982; IPCC 2006 Vol.1 Ch.3 §3.2.3.2). Each parameter is sampled from its specified marginal distribution (PERT, normal, lognormal, beta, etc. — as set in the Parameters sheet), and the samples are then re-ordered so that the rank correlation across pairs matches the target. The marginal distribution of each parameter is preserved exactly; only the joint pattern of ranks changes.

The following panels show two parameters with identical right-skewed Beta distributions, sampled independently (left) and with rank correlation ρ = −0.5 imposed via Iman–Conover (right):

The right-hand cloud tilts downward, but the lopsided shape of each marginal axis is unchanged. This is the defining property of the Iman–Conover procedure: marginal distributions are preserved exactly, and only the joint pattern of ranks is modified.

Implications for the IPCC equations

Several pairs of IPCC Tier 2 input parameters are biologically correlated. The most pronounced is between digestible energy (DE) and the methane conversion factor Ym: when feed quality rises (higher DE), less of the ingested energy is lost as methane (lower Ym); when feed quality falls, Ym rises. The two parameters enter the enteric-CH4 equation as a product, so the joint behaviour of DE × Ym directly shapes the emissions distribution.

When DE and Ym are sampled independently, a non-trivial share of Monte Carlo iterations falls on combinations that are not biologically plausible — for example, very high DE paired with very high Ym. The product is then artificially inflated in those iterations, widening the right tail of the resulting CH4 distribution.

Imposing ρ = −0.5 between DE and Ym excludes the implausible combinations and changes the shape of the product distribution as follows:

Two observations follow from the figure:

First, the means of the two histograms (dashed vertical lines) are essentially identical. By construction, the Iman–Conover procedure preserves each marginal distribution, including its mean, so imposing correlation does not shift the central estimate of any function of those parameters.

Second, the right-hand histogram is narrower. The negative correlation suppresses the implausible high-DE/high-Ym combinations that would otherwise produce extreme values of the product, reducing the spread. Positive correlations between parameters that enter a product have the opposite effect: they widen the spread by clustering extremes in the same direction.

Within the full IPCC Tier 2 equation chain, the structural-defaults preset applies seven such correlations alongside roughly a dozen independent inputs. The individual effects partially cancel, and the net result on the 95% margin of error of total CO2eq is typically a few percentage points. The mean of the total is preserved.

Country X (smallholder dairy)

Country X is the built-in dairy example: 500,000 head, milking cows on improved pasture and concentrate supplement, with DE ≈ 55%, Milk ≈ 4 kg per lactating cow per day, and Ym ≈ 6.5%. Diet quality and methane conversion both vary substantially across the herd, which is the condition under which the DE ↔︎ Ym correlation has the largest effect on the simulated emissions distribution.

The seven pairs in the structural-defaults preset

The “Structural defaults” mode applies seven parameter-pair correlations drawn from published livestock-science literature. Each pair, its assigned ρ value, and its source are listed below:

  • BW ↔︎ MW (ρ = +0.50). Cattle with above-average adult body weight typically also exceeded the average at younger ages, following standard allometric growth curves (Brody 1945). Used in IPCC Vol.4 Ch.10 Eq 10.3. The value 0.50 reflects the typical case in which BW and MW come from different sources (BW from a livestock census, MW from a breed reference); when both come from the same animal-condition survey, a higher value can be set via the manual matrix.

  • BW ↔︎ WG (ρ = +0.40). In well-fed adult cattle (dairy or beef finishing systems), heavier animals tend to gain weight more rapidly. NRC (2001) joint regression of body weight and average daily gain. The sign of this correlation is system-dependent: in growing-young-stock systems with feed limitation, BW and WG can be negatively correlated.

  • Milk ↔︎ Fat (ρ = −0.30). The dilution effect: higher daily milk yield is associated with lower fat content per litre. Reported across the dairy genetics literature (Wilmink 1987; VandeHaar 1998), with within-herd values typically between −0.20 and −0.40.

  • Milk ↔︎ BW (ρ = +0.30). High-producing dairy breeds are physically larger (e.g. Holstein ~650 kg, ~30 kg/day vs Jersey ~450 kg, ~20 kg/day). NRC Dairy (2001).

  • Milk ↔︎ DE (ρ = +0.30). Higher feed digestibility increases nutrient availability, which lifts milk yield. The effect is strongest in concentrate-fed dairy systems. NRC Dairy (2001) Ch. 2.

  • DE ↔︎ CP (ρ = +0.50). Feed-quality co-variation: high-quality forages tend to have both high digestibility and high crude protein. Cross-feedstuff correlations of 0.4–0.7 are documented in NRC, INRA, and Feedipedia.

  • DE ↔︎ Ym (ρ = −0.50). The largest-magnitude correlation in the preset and the dominant contributor to the headline MoE shift in dairy systems. IPCC 2019 Refinement Eq 10.21 expresses Ym as an explicit decreasing function of DE; observational meta-analyses (Niu et al. 2018; Hristov et al. 2013) report cross-diet slopes consistent with ρ ≈ −0.50.

Selecting an activity-data correlation mode

The left-hand card on the Simulate tab offers four options:

No correlations. The strict IPCC Approach-2 default, applicable when activity-data sources are independent or when a conservative baseline is required.

Structural defaults (expert-elicited). Applies the seven biological pairs documented above. Suitable for single-year inventories without a multi-year time series of feed quality or herd structure.

From template (auto, time-series). Computes a Spearman rank-correlation matrix from the user’s Parameter_TimeSeries sheet, with first-difference detrending applied internally to remove shared long-run growth. Requires at least five years of populated data; the radio button is disabled in the UI when this condition is not met.

Manual (CSV upload). Uses a user-supplied square correlation matrix, intended for cases where a published matrix from a similar inventory or an expert-elicitation result is available. A template is provided in the panel.

Emission-factor correlations (right-hand card)

The Simulate tab includes two correlation cards. The left-hand card, described above, controls correlations between activity-data parameters (herd size, body weight, milk yield, feed quality — quantities measured from the population). The right-hand card controls correlations between emission-factor coefficients (Ym, Bo, MCF, EF3, EF4, EF5 — fixed quantities sourced from published studies rather than from a per-population survey).

The two cards are separated because the underlying mechanisms differ. Activity-data correlations arise from biological co-variation across animals and feed. Emission-factor correlations arise from shared methodological bias in the published values — for example, when several coefficients are calibrated against a common regional dataset.

The three coefficient blocks

The right-hand card groups the IPCC coefficients into three blocks based on the scientific literature they originate from. A within-block correlation ρ can be applied to represent shared bias within a block; correlation across blocks is fixed at zero on the assumption that the three literatures are independent.

Block Coefficients Common literature source
Energy Cfi, Ca, C, Cp, Ym Rumen-fermentation and animal-energetics studies (IPCC Vol.4 Ch.10 §10.2).
Manure-CH4 Bo, MCF, ASH Biogas-potential and lagoon studies.
Manure-N EF3, EF4, EF5, Frac_GasMS, Frac_LeachMS, UE Soil-emissions and N-volatilisation studies.

Behaviour of the within-block ρ slider

When “Block-structured EF correlation” is selected and the energy-block slider is set to ρ = 0.3, the sampler treats the five energy coefficients as sharing a common bias term of moderate strength. An iteration in which Ym samples above its central value will also draw Cfi slightly above its central value (and similarly for the other energy coefficients), with the magnitude of the offset proportional to ρ.

The figure below shows the joint sample cloud of Ym and Cfi under three slider settings:

At ρ = 0, the sample cloud is approximately circular, indicating independent sampling of Ym and Cfi. At ρ = 0.3 a mild upward tilt is visible, and at ρ = 0.5 the tilt is pronounced. The same procedure is applied uniformly to all coefficients in the block.

Applying the energy-block EF correlation alone (ρ = 0.3) increases the margin of error by approximately two percentage points relative to the no-correlation baseline; ρ = 0.5 produces a larger increase. Guidance on when to enable EF correlations

The block-structured EF correlation is appropriate only when there is reason to believe that several emission-factor coefficients share a common methodological bias. The IPCC defaults are aggregated across many independent studies, so no single bias dominates; when the user’s emission factors are taken from IPCC defaults, the EF correlation should remain off. Similarly, if the emission factors are sourced from country-specific measurements performed at different laboratories or in different years, the corresponding errors can be treated as independent.

EF correlation at ρ = 0.3 is reasonable when several coefficients within a block originate from a single methodological source — for example, when all energy-equation coefficients (Cfi, Ca, Ym, and others) are drawn from a single national feeding trial or a single regional rumen database. The slider interpretation displayed under each slider categorises ρ in four bands: 0 (independent), up to 0.15 (weak shared bias), 0.16 to 0.35 (moderate shared bias), and above 0.35 (strong shared bias requiring documented justification).

The same reasoning applies to the manure-CH4 block (Bo, MCF, ASH) and the manure-N block (EF3, EF4, EF5, Frac_GasMS, Frac_LeachMS, UE): engage a non-zero ρ only when the constituent coefficients are known to share a common source.

If the “Block-structured EF correlation” mode is selected but all three sliders are left at zero, an amber warning appears under the sliders. In this configuration the simulation behaves identically to the “No EF correlations” mode; the warning indicates that the user should either move at least one slider above zero or switch back to the default mode.

Common pitfalls

  • Expecting the mean to change. Correlations modify the spread of the emissions distribution but preserve the central estimate to within Monte Carlo sampling noise. A mean shift of more than a few tenths of a percent across correlation modes is typically not a correlation effect but a separate issue (most often related to parameter rebroadcasting).

  • Selecting “From template (auto)” with an empty Parameter_TimeSeries sheet. In earlier versions this configuration silently reverted to no correlations. The radio button is now disabled when the sheet contains no data, and the Run button blocks with an explicit error if stale state slips through. Identical heights for the green and grey bars in the comparison chart are a symptom of this configuration.

  • Selecting “Block-structured EF correlation” with all three sliders at zero. This produces a no-op identical to the default mode. An amber warning appears under the sliders; the user should move at least one slider above zero or switch back to “No EF correlations”.

  • Modifying row or column headers in the manual matrix. Names that do not match the canonical entries in PARAM_CATALOGUE are dropped silently during import. The example template provided in the panel contains the canonical names and should be used as the starting point.

  • Concerns about positive-definiteness. If the uploaded matrix is not positive-definite, the tool automatically projects it onto the nearest positive-definite correlation matrix and reports any entry that shifts by more than 0.01. Manual construction of a positive-definite matrix is not required.

  • Interpreting a small headline effect as an error. A single correlated pair within a multiplicative chain of approximately thirteen inputs typically shifts the 95% margin of error by only a few percent. This is the expected mathematical behaviour. Larger effects require either a richer multi-year time series (the “From template (auto)” mode aggregates many correlated pairs simultaneously) or genuinely wide per-parameter uncertainty bounds.

Evaluating the effect on a user-supplied inventory

Selecting the “Compare with/without correlations” option on the Simulate tab before running the simulation produces a side-by-side bar chart on the Results tab titled “Effect of Correlations on Uncertainty”. The green bars show the 95% margin of error under the selected correlation configuration, and the grey bars show the same simulation with all correlations disabled. The difference between the two bars represents the effect of correlations on the user’s specific inventory.

The Country X and Country Y examples in this document illustrate the typical magnitude of this effect, but the precise gap depends on the per-parameter uncertainty bounds and the sub-category composition of the inventory under analysis.
