Correlations — Find out more

Quick answer. If you’re unsure, pick No correlations — most national inventories do. If your input parameters are clearly linked physically (e.g. high-fibre diets push Ym up and DE down), pick Structural defaults for a more honest representation. If you have ≥5 years of historical inventory data, pick From template (auto) — most accurate. Pick Manual entry only if you have a correlation matrix from a published study or expert elicitation.

Why correlations matter at all

Monte Carlo without correlations draws each input parameter independently each iteration. That means an iteration can land on physically-implausible combinations — for example, a draw where DE = 60% (high-quality feed) and Ym = 7.5% (high methane conversion, which IPCC associates with low-quality feed). In reality those two values almost never co-occur in the same animal.

Adding a correlation between DE and Ym tells the sampler “when one is high, the other tends to be low”. Iterations now cluster around the physically-plausible combinations and the tails of the emission distribution shift.

Key effect on the headline: correlations widen the 95% confidence interval, not narrow it. The strongly-linked extremes (high-DE / low-Ym at one end, low-DE / high-Ym at the other) become more likely than independent sampling makes them, and these extremes produce more extreme emission totals. Correlations do not bias the central estimate — the mean is preserved because correlations operate on the joint distribution, not the marginals.

Decision flow

Do you have a Parameter_TimeSeries sheet with ≥5 years of data?
        │
        ├── YES → "From template (auto, time-series)" — RECOMMENDED
        │
        └── NO  → Do you believe your input parameters move together
                  (e.g. heavier cattle eat more — BW & MW correlated)?
                       │
                       ├── YES → "Structural defaults (expert-elicited)"
                       │
                       └── NO  → "No correlations"
                                  (always a safe, IPCC-standard default)

Do you have a correlation matrix from a published study or expert elicitation?
        │
        └── If YES → "Advanced — manual entry" with a CSV upload

The four options compared

Option What it does When to pick Data needed
No correlations All parameters sampled independently each iteration Default. You have no information about co-movement. None
Structural defaults Applies 7 expert-elicited pairs from the livestock literature: BW↔︎MW, BW↔︎WG, Milk↔︎Fat, Milk↔︎BW, Milk↔︎DE, DE↔︎CP, DE↔︎Ym Single-year inventory with no historical data; you want the most well-documented links applied automatically. None — built into the tool.
From template (auto, time-series) Computes a Spearman rank-correlation matrix from your Parameter_TimeSeries sheet (after first-difference detrending). You have ≥5 years of historical inventory data with non-constant parameters. A populated Parameter_TimeSeries sheet (≥5 rows, ≥2 numeric columns).
Manual entry Use a square CSV correlation matrix you supply. You have a published correlation matrix from a similar inventory, or expert-elicitation results. A CSV — downloadable templates are inside the panel.

What’s in the structural-defaults preset?

The “Structural defaults” mode applies seven pairs drawn from the IPCC equation structure and the livestock literature. All other pairs are zero.

Each non-zero cell is documented in the methodology. The values:

Pair ρ Source / rationale (summary)
BW ↔︎ MW +0.50 Growth-curve relation (Brody 1945); IPCC Eq 10.3. Set to 0.5 rather than 0.85 to reflect typical mixed-source provenance (BW from census, MW from breed reference).
BW ↔︎ WG +0.40 IPCC Eq 10.6 joint estimation; NRC 2001. Sign system-dependent; +0.40 reflects well-fed dairy/beef.
Milk ↔︎ Fat −0.30 Milk dilution effect, dairy genetics literature (Wilmink 1987; VandeHaar 1998).
Milk ↔︎ BW +0.30 High-producing dairy breeds are physically larger. NRC Dairy 2001.
Milk ↔︎ DE +0.30 Higher feed digestibility → higher milk yield, especially concentrate-fed.
DE ↔︎ CP +0.50 Forage-quality co-variation across feed types (NRC; INRA).
DE ↔︎ Ym −0.50 IPCC 2019 Refinement Eq 10.21 + meta-analysis (Niu 2018, Hristov 2013). Cross-block — the dominant headline mover.

Common mistakes

  • Expecting the mean to change. It doesn’t. Correlations widen the CI; they don’t bias the central estimate.

  • Renaming row/column headers in the manual matrix. Names that don’t match the canonical PARAM_CATALOGUE are silently dropped — the simulation runs but the correlation doesn’t apply. Always start from the downloadable example template, which ships with the canonical names already in place.

  • Picking “From template (auto)” without 5 years of time-series data. The radio greys out automatically when the prerequisite is missing, but if you switch upload files mid-session the state may persist briefly — re-check that the selected mode matches your data.

  • Worrying about positive-definiteness. If your uploaded matrix is not positive-definite, the tool projects it onto the nearest positive-definite correlation matrix via Matrix::nearPD() and warns you if any entry shifts by more than 0.01. You don’t need to construct a perfectly valid matrix by hand.

  • Missing parameters. If your uploaded matrix only covers, say, 10 parameters, the simulation treats all pairs involving the missing 15 as independent (zero correlation). That’s intentional graceful degradation, not an error.
