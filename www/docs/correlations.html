Parameter Correlations in Monte Carlo GHG Inventory Uncertainty Analysis | Cattle GHG Uncertainty Calculator Help

This page describes how correlations between input parameters affect Monte Carlo simulation results in the tool, what the correlation settings on the Simulate tab control, and how to interpret each option using two worked examples.

The principle: when two input parameters tend to move together in nature, the Monte Carlo sampler should reproduce that joint behaviour. Sampling them independently when they are correlated produces parameter combinations that do not occur in real systems, which biases the spread (though not the central estimate) of the simulated emissions distribution.

Visualising correlation

Consider two parameters, with 1000 Monte Carlo samples of each, plotted as a scatter cloud: one point per iteration, the first parameter on the x-axis and the second on the y-axis. The three panels below show the same two marginal distributions under different correlation strengths:

In the centre panel, when parameter 1 takes a high value, parameter 2 tends to be high as well. The cloud tilts upward. In the right-hand panel the relationship is reversed: high values of parameter 1 occur with low values of parameter 2, and the cloud tilts downward.

The marginal distribution of each parameter is identical across all three panels: the same range on each axis. Correlation does not change the distribution of either parameter on its own; it changes only which combinations of values occur together.

Preserving marginal distributions (Iman–Conover method)

Real inventory parameters rarely follow symmetric normal distributions. Body weight is approximately symmetric, milk yield has a long right tail, and the methane conversion factor Ym is bounded between approximately 5% and 8%. A bivariate-normal sampler with a target correlation cannot reproduce these shapes.

The tool uses the Iman–Conover procedure (Iman & Conover 1982; IPCC 2006 Vol.1 Ch.3 §3.2.3.2). Each parameter is sampled from its specified marginal distribution (PERT, normal, lognormal, beta, etc.: as set in the Parameters sheet), and the samples are then re-ordered so that the rank correlation across pairs matches the target. The marginal distribution of each parameter is preserved exactly; only the joint pattern of ranks changes.

The following panels show two parameters with identical right-skewed Beta distributions, sampled independently (left) and with rank correlation ρ = −0.5 imposed via Iman–Conover (right):

The right-hand cloud tilts downward, but the lopsided shape of each marginal axis is unchanged. This is the defining property of the Iman–Conover procedure: marginal distributions are preserved exactly, and only the joint pattern of ranks is modified.

Implications for the IPCC equations

Several pairs of IPCC Tier 2 input parameters are biologically correlated. A familiar example is the link between digestible energy (DE) and the methane conversion factor Ym: the IPCC 2019 Refinement (Table 10.12) assigns a lower Ym to more digestible diets. The two parameters enter the enteric-CH4 equation as a product, so the joint behaviour of DE × Ym directly shapes the emissions distribution.

When DE and Ym are sampled independently, a non-trivial share of Monte Carlo iterations falls on combinations that are not biologically plausible: for example, very high DE paired with very high Ym. The product is then artificially inflated in those iterations, widening the right tail of the resulting CH4 distribution.

For illustration, imposing a strong negative correlation (ρ = −0.5) between DE and Ym excludes the implausible combinations and changes the shape of the product distribution as follows (the preset itself uses −0.20 for this pair, see the seven pairs below):

Two observations follow from the figure:

First, the means of the two histograms (dashed vertical lines) are essentially identical. By construction, the Iman–Conover procedure preserves each marginal distribution, including its mean, so imposing correlation does not shift the central estimate of any function of those parameters.

Second, the right-hand histogram is narrower. The negative correlation suppresses the implausible high-DE/high-Ym combinations that would otherwise produce extreme values of the product, reducing the spread. Positive correlations between parameters that enter a product have the opposite effect: they widen the spread by clustering extremes in the same direction.

Within the full IPCC Tier 2 equation chain, the structural-defaults preset applies seven such correlations alongside roughly a dozen independent inputs. The individual effects partially cancel, and the net result on the 95% margin of error of total CO2eq is typically a few percentage points. The mean of the total is preserved.

The seven pairs in the structural-defaults preset

The “Structural defaults” mode applies seven parameter-pair correlations. The values are literature-based: the direction of each link comes from the IPCC equations and published studies, and the size was set by the project team from the evidence listed; the IPCC publishes no numerical correlation coefficients for these pairs. Each pair, its ρ value, the biological reason and the references are listed below (the same text appears when you hover a cell of the heatmap in the app):

  • BW ↔︎ MW (ρ = +0.50). Live weight and mature weight enter the IPCC net-energy-for-growth equation together, and animals of larger breeds are heavier at every age and have a larger mature weight. Set below the same-survey value because MW is usually a breed-reference constant while BW comes from the census; when both come from the same survey, 0.8 to 0.9 can be set via the manual matrix. IPCC 2006 Eq 10.6; IPCC 2019 Table 10A.2.

  • BW ↔︎ WG (ρ = +0.40). Live weight and daily gain enter the same equation, and well-fed animals are both heavier and gain faster; the sign can reverse for young stock approaching mature size. IPCC 2006 Eq 10.6; Mehrban et al. 2021 (Animal Bioscience 34:824–832, phenotypic correlation 0.49 to 0.63 in 15,279 cattle); IPCC 2019 Table 10A.2.

  • Milk ↔︎ Fat (ρ = −0.30). Dilution effect: as milk yield rises, fat yield rises less than proportionally, so the fat percentage falls. Boujenane 2002 (−0.28 in 20,140 Holstein-Friesian lactations); Nistor et al. 2011 (−0.26 in 120 cows); IPCC 2019 Table 10A.1 (−0.30 across nine regions).

  • Milk ↔︎ BW (ρ = +0.30). Larger dairy breeds and more intensive systems have both heavier cows and higher milk yields; within one breed the link is weak. IPCC 2019 Table 10A.1 (0.84 across regions); Berry and Evans 2022 (JDS Communications 3:32–37, the heaviest quarter of 16,980 cows yields only 3 to 4% more); Berry et al. 2003 (J. Dairy Sci. 86:2193–2204). A single-breed herd may use 0.10 to 0.20.

  • Milk ↔︎ DE (ρ = +0.30). More digestible feed gives more net energy per kg of feed and supports higher milk yield. NRC 2001 Ch. 2 (Energy); IPCC 2019 Table 10A.1 (0.65 across regions); no within-system coefficient is published.

  • DE ↔︎ CP (ρ = +0.20). Within one forage, digestibility and crude protein fall together as the plant matures; across different feeds the link is weak, because protein and digestibility depend on different things (plant family, plant part, processing). INRAE-CIRAD-AFZ feed tables 2018 (feedtables.com: r = −0.07 over 193 plant feed materials); Feedipedia (0.1 to 0.2 over 82 forages with measured digestibility). Use 0.3 to 0.5 when the uncertainty is mainly about forage maturity.

  • DE ↔︎ Ym (ρ = −0.20). IPCC Table 10.12 assigns a lower Ym to more digestible diets, mainly because high digestibility goes with high feeding level and grain-rich rations, which shift rumen fermentation and speed up passage; within forage-based diets, methane per unit of energy changes little with digestibility or rises slightly. IPCC 2019 Table 10.12; Ramin and Huhtanen 2013 (J. Dairy Sci. 96:2476–2493); Johnson and Johnson 1995 (J. Anim. Sci. 73:2483–2492). The value suits forage-based systems; use −0.3 to −0.5 for high-grain dairy or feedlot systems.

Two different correlations, and which one matters more

This page describes two settings that are both called correlation and are easy to confuse.

The correlation mode covers relationships between different parameters within a group: body weight with mature weight, digestibility with methane conversion, and so on. Its effect on the headline figure is real but modest, typically widening the interval by 3 to 10 per cent on a real inventory.

The same parameter reused across groups is a different matter. If one IPCC default or one regional study supplies a value used by many groups, that is a single estimate applied repeatedly. The tool draws such a value once per iteration for every group that carries it, and draws groups with their own value separately; nothing needs to be set. The correlation mode chosen on this page is applied between two shared values when they cover the same groups (one DE and one Ym used by the same groups move together), and between a group’s own values; a shared value cannot be linked to a value that differs from group to group, because one draw cannot follow several. This is the larger of the two effects: on the Zambia inventory (32 groups) the reported margin of error is 32.5 per cent with shared draws and would be 23.7 per cent if every group drew its own value, and on the test in Common pitfalls below the difference grows with the number of groups.

Selecting an activity-data correlation mode

The left-hand card on the Simulate tab offers four options:

No correlations. The strict IPCC Approach-2 default, applicable when activity-data sources are independent or when a conservative baseline is required.

Structural defaults (literature-based). Applies the seven biological pairs documented above. Suitable for single-year inventories without a multi-year time series of feed quality or herd structure.

From template (auto, time-series). Keeps the seven literature pairs and lets the user’s Parameter_TimeSeries sheet set the size of a pair where the series is long and clear: ten or more years, a Spearman rank correlation of the year-to-year changes with p below 0.05, and the literature’s sign. Otherwise the pair keeps its literature value, and no other pair is created. A check under the option shows, pair by pair, what was used and why; if nothing passes, the run uses the structural defaults and says so. (Until 30 October 2026 this mode applied a correlation between every two columns of the sheet, which with a short series is mostly chance.)

Manual (CSV upload). Uses a user-supplied square correlation matrix, intended for cases where a published matrix from a similar inventory or an expert-elicitation result is available. A template is provided in the panel.

Common pitfalls

  • Assuming a smaller margin of error means a better inventory. It can mean the opposite. If the same coefficient value is reused across many groups and each group drew it separately, the errors would cancel against each other and the reported uncertainty would shrink as the inventory is disaggregated more finely: holding the herd, the parameters and the uncertainty inputs completely fixed and varying only the number of groups gives 25.1% on one group, 12.5% on four, 6.3% on sixteen, 3.1% on sixty-four and 1.5% on 251, with the data never changing. The tool avoids this by drawing a shared value once for every group that uses it (on the same test the figure stays between 25.1% and 22.7%). Still compare results only at the same level of disaggregation, and report the level you used.

  • Expecting the mean to change. Correlations modify the spread of the emissions distribution but preserve the central estimate to within Monte Carlo sampling noise. A mean shift of more than a few tenths of a percent across correlation modes is typically not a correlation effect but a separate issue (most often related to parameter rebroadcasting).

  • Selecting “From template (auto)” with an empty Parameter_TimeSeries sheet. In earlier versions this configuration silently reverted to no correlations. The radio button is now disabled when the sheet contains no data, and the Run button blocks with an explicit error if stale state slips through. Identical heights for the green and grey bars in the comparison chart are a symptom of this configuration.

  • Modifying row or column headers in the manual matrix. Names that do not match the canonical entries in PARAM_CATALOGUE are dropped silently during import. The example template provided in the panel contains the canonical names and should be used as the starting point.

  • Concerns about positive-definiteness. If the uploaded matrix is not positive-definite, the tool automatically projects it onto the nearest positive-definite correlation matrix and reports any entry that shifts by more than 0.01. Manual construction of a positive-definite matrix is not required.

  • Interpreting a small headline effect as an error. A single correlated pair within a multiplicative chain of approximately thirteen inputs typically shifts the 95% margin of error by only a few percent. This is the expected mathematical behaviour. Larger effects require either a richer multi-year time series (the “From template (auto)” mode aggregates many correlated pairs simultaneously) or genuinely wide per-parameter uncertainty bounds.

Evaluating the effect on a user-supplied inventory

Selecting the “Compare with/without correlations” option on the Simulate tab before running the simulation produces a side-by-side bar chart on the Results tab titled “Effect of Correlations on Uncertainty”. The green bars show the 95% margin of error under the selected correlation configuration, and the grey bars show the same simulation with all correlations disabled. The difference between the two bars represents the effect of correlations on the user’s specific inventory.

The Country X and Country Y examples in this document illustrate the typical magnitude of this effect, but the precise gap depends on the per-parameter uncertainty bounds and the sub-category composition of the inventory under analysis.
