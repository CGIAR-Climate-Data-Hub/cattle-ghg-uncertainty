Tier 2 enteric fermentation uncertainty: what drives it

How does the Tier 2 method estimate enteric CH4?

The IPCC Tier 2 method (2006 Guidelines, Vol. 4, Ch. 10) builds each animal category's gross energy intake (GE) from an energy balance: maintenance (a function of body weight), activity, growth, lactation, work and pregnancy, scaled by the diet's digestible energy fraction (DE). A methane conversion factor (Ym, the share of gross energy lost as methane) turns GE into an emission factor per head, and the population scales it to the national total. Every one of those inputs carries uncertainty, and because they multiply through the chain, their uncertainties compound.

Which parameters dominate the uncertainty?

Sensitivity analyses of Tier 2 cattle inventories repeatedly point to the same short list:

  • Animal population (N). The one true activity-data input; it scales everything linearly, so its uncertainty passes straight through to the total.
  • Body weight (BW). Drives maintenance energy through a power function (BW to the 0.75), so it punches above its nominal uncertainty.
  • Digestible energy (DE). Enters the chain in several places at once (energy availability, feed intake, and through it nitrogen excretion), and is one of the hardest parameters to survey.
  • Methane conversion factor (Ym). Multiplies the emission factor directly, and its IPCC default uncertainty is wide; Ym is also biologically linked to DE (better diets tend to lower Ym), a correlation an honest analysis should represent.
  • Milk yield and growth rates in dairy and fattening systems respectively.

Which of these dominates in your inventory depends on your data quality, which is exactly what a sensitivity ranking (tornado chart) tells you: it separates the parameters you can improve with better local data from the fixed IPCC coefficients you cannot.

How large is the uncertainty typically?

With IPCC default uncertainty ranges, combined 95% uncertainty on Tier 2 enteric CH4 commonly lands between roughly 15% and 40% of the mean, wider where population statistics are weak or diets are poorly characterised. Because several inputs are skewed and the equation chain is non-linear, IPCC Approach 2 (Monte Carlo) gives materially more reliable intervals than Approach 1 error propagation.
